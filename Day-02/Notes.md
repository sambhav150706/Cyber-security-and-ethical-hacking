Day 02: Types of Hackers & Careers
Cybersecurity Track · Beginner Level · 4 November 2026
1. Fundamental Concept: What is a Hacker?
A hacker is an individual who leverages specialized technical knowledge to understand, test, manipulate, or
gain access to computer systems and network architectures. Their underlying intentions and explicit
authorization determine whether their actions fall under ethical security operations or criminal activity.
2. Three Major Types of Hackers
1. White Hat Hacker (Ethical Hacker)
Tests systems with explicit prior authorization to identify vulnerabilities, document risks, and help organizations
patch flaws before malicious actors discover them. Example: A penetration tester hired by a financial institution.
2. Black Hat Hacker (Malicious Hacker)
Breaches networks and endpoints without consent for malicious, illegal, or financial motives—including data
exfiltration, ransomware deployment, or service disruption. Example: An attacker stealing credential databases.
3. Grey Hat Hacker
Discovers flaws without permission, but lacks destructive intent. They may later report the bug to the system
owner. Crucial legal rule: Even with harmless intentions, accessing systems without written authorization is
illegal.
Rule of Thumb: White Hat = Authorized & Ethical | Black Hat = Malicious & Unauthorized | Grey Hat = Unsolicited
testing without permission (still illegal).
3. Ethical Hacking vs Malicious Hacking
Ethical Hacking (White Hat)
Explicit written permission obtained
Defined scope, rules of engagement, and schedules
Discovers vulnerabilities to help remediate them
Findings reported responsibly to owners
Objective: Bolster organizational defense posture
Malicious Hacking (Black Hat)
Zero authorization obtained
Unrestricted, unauthorized access
Exploits vulnerabilities for leverage or profit
Data stolen, published, sold, or held for ransom
Objective: Cause harm, extort, or gain illicit benefit
Warning: A personal claim of "good intentions" never legitimizes unauthorized probing or scanning. Always stay
strictly within designated scopes on authorized lab systems.
4. Key Cybersecurity Career Paths
SOC Analyst (Tier 1 / Tier 2)
Monitors real-time SIEM alerts, investigates suspicious endpoint activity, triages malicious traffic, and performs
initial incident triage. Core skills: Networking, Linux, SIEM dashboards, packet analysis.
Penetration Tester (Offensive Security)
Conducts authorized security assessments to simulate real-world attacks, discover logic flaws, and evaluate
defensive controls. Core skills: Networking, web application exploits, scripting (Python/Bash).
Digital Forensics Analyst (DFIR)
Preserves, analyzes, and reconstructs digital evidence following cyber incidents to explain breaches and prepare
findings for legal proceedings. Core skills: File systems, memory analysis, OS artifacts.
Application Security Engineer (AppSec)
Collaborates directly with software developers to review source code, prevent OWASP Top 10 vulnerabilities, and
establish secure SDLC pipelines. Core skills: Secure code review, APIs, threat modeling.
5. Foundational Skills Checklist & Core Takeaway
Core Competencies: Networking (IP, DNS, ports, protocols), Linux system administration, Python
automation, and Web technologies (HTTP/HTTPS, APIs).
Day 02 Takeaway: You do not become a cybersecurity professional simply by running automated hacking
tools. Focus on deep systems fundamentals, lab isolation, legal ethics, and defensive architectures.
